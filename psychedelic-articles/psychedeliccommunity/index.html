What Happens After the Medicine

Written By Linden Schaffer

Sitting with psychedelics isn't the work. Your relationship to the medicine, to yourself, and to the process is.

I spent this weekend at Chacruna Institute's Psychedelic Culture conference and I left thinking about what we're still not fully saying out loud. I'm not talking only about our relationship to a substance. I'm talking about our relationship to ourselves. To other people. To community. And to the systems we return to when the experience is over.

Because here's what kept echoing across panels, conversations, and the quieter moments in between:

Psychedelics can create insight. But without integration, insight doesn't necessarily become change.

And change doesn't happen in isolation.
It happens in relationship.
In community.
In environments that can actually hold what gets uncovered.

The experience isn't the transformation.

What has struck me most in the six years I've been navigating this field is how many people are having profound experiences and then returning to the exact same conditions that shaped their pain in the first place.

They're having the experience, the realization, the holy shit, I finally understand why I do this moment.

And then they go home.

Back to the same relationship, job, nervous system patterns, social environment. The same expectations. The same life. I've been one of them.

Psychedelic tourism isn't just about traveling somewhere to take something. It's about treating the experience itself as the destination. But insight isn't the same thing as transformation. And gaining awareness without support can become its own kind of overwhelm.

What happens when the experience ends?

This is the part I think we need to get much more interested in.

Not just: What did you experience? But: What are you going to do differently because of it?

What relationships need to change? What boundaries need to be built? What truths need to be spoken? What support do you need? What environment are you returning to? What are you willing to stop doing? What are you finally willing to begin?

Because sometimes the most profound psychedelic experience in the world can't compensate for a life that has no room for what it revealed.

The container matters.

Before, during, and especially after.

And then there's the bigger conversation.

There was another tension running through the conference that deserves far more than one article: What happens when a field grows faster than its systems can responsibly hold it?

We are watching psychedelics, investment, clinical applications and access all scale. But scale isn't inherently synonymous with progress.

What happens when we prioritize speed over safety? When we build systems that can't actually support transformation? When commercialization moves faster than accountability?

When we talk about decolonization while Indigenous knowledge is still treated as an aesthetic, an origin story, or intellectual property rather than living knowledge held within communities and lineages?

When a field continues to grow without asking:

Who benefits?
Who bears the cost?
Who gave permission?
What does reciprocity actually look like?

These aren't side conversations. They're part of the relationship.

So I'm sitting with a different set of questions. What is my relationship to the medicine, to myself, the process, community, to the systems I'm participating in? And perhaps most importantly: How do I build relationships capable of holding truth, discomfort, and repair?

Because that's where I think the real work begins. Not in chasing the next profound experience. Not in collecting insights. Not in finding the perfect ceremony, facilitator, substance, or setting.

But in building a life, relationships, and communities capable of holding what we've learned.

That's what actually creates change. That's the real medicine.

Thank you to Maestras Laura and Edelin for so generously inviting people into the conversation, and for the gift of sharing their family, culture, lineage, and presence.
